# War Diary — 3rd Canadian Engineers Reserve Battalion — June 1919

- **Unit:** 3rd Canadian Engineers Reserve Battalion (3rd C.E.R.B.)
- **Month:** June 1919 — 1 June to 30 June 1919 (final month of the diary)
- **Source:** Library and Archives Canada, Collection Search Item ID 2004925 (RG 9 III-D-3, vol. 5008, file 705, reel T-10856, finding aid 9-52)
- **Pages:** June 1919, p. 1 – p. 11 (image files page-0050 – page-0060)
- **June 1919 — COMPLETE** (pages 1–11): cover, diary text 1–30 June, and three appendices — Movement Order Ref. No. 22-D-516 of 13 June 1919 (1st & 18th Reserve Sailings, 14 June), Movement Orders for Sailing No. 71, S.S. "Belgic" (battalion sailing, 23 June), and Special Order of the Day by Lieut-General Sir R.W. Turner, 20 June 1919. Transcribed 2026-10-27.

## Transcription conventions
Original spelling, punctuation and abbreviations kept. Diary text is typewritten throughout (Army Form C. 2118, "WAR DIARY or INTELLIGENCE SUMMARY" — "INTELLIGENCE SUMMARY" not struck through on these sheets; the printed heading reads "WAR DIARY or INTELLIGENCE SUMMARY" with "(Erase heading not required.)" beneath). `[uncertain]` marks words I could not read with confidence — never guessed; struck-through text is shown as [struck through — appears to read "..."]. Handwritten additions to the typed sheets are noted as [handwritten]. The "Remarks and references to Appendices" column bears a handwritten initial (appears "Ah") on each row. "O.R." = other ranks; "G.O.C." = General Officer Commanding; "G.C.M." = General Court Martial; "V.C." = Victoria Cross; "C.E." = Canadian Engineers; "C.E.T.C." = Canadian Engineers Training Centre; "S.S." = steamship; "Oval" = the sports ground at Seaford used as the embarkation assembly point. Dates are written "1-6-19" etc. Place column reads "Seaford" throughout the diary sheets. The Hour column is blank throughout. "Picquet" is the diary's spelling of picket. The two movement-order appendices are faint carbon copies with show-through from the reverse of the sheets; where the type is illegible this is noted. June 1919 is the battalion's last month: the main body sails for Canada on S.S. Belgic on 23 June, and on 30 June "3rd C.E.R.B. ceases to exist as a Unit in England."

## Page 11 — Appendix: Special Order of the Day, 20 June 1919

A single typewritten sheet — a Special Order of the Day by Lieut-General Sir R.W. Turner V.C. K.C.B. K.C.M.G. D.S.O., Chief of General Staff, Overseas Military Forces of Canada, dated 20th June 1919, on the rioting at Witley Camp and the disturbance at Epsom.

> SPECIAL ORDER OF THE DAY
> By
> Lieut-General Sir R.W. Turner V.C. K.C.B. K.C.M.G. D.S.O.
> Chief of General Staff
> OVERSEAS MILITARY FORCES OF CANADA.
>
> 20th June 1919.
>
> With deep regret I feel compelled to refer to certain disturbances which have recently occurred.
> All ranks are aware of the senseless acts of rioting which took place at Witley Camp during the last week. Fortunately the better element in the Camp prevailed and the trouble is now at an end. Had those elements only recognized earlier the seriousness of the situation, or, had they at the commencement of the trouble realized the consequences of inaction, they would never have permitted the disturbance to reach the dimensions it did. I believe that they have learnt their lesson and that they will join with promptness and vigour in the suppression of any further outbreak of this nature in the Area.
> A more serious and shameful disturbance has since occurred at Epsom, the details of which are known to all. For such a disturbance there can be no conceivable or possible excuse. It is a piece of criminal folly which every decent Canadian will condemn and regret.
> Those disturbances were, in certain respects, similar. In both instances the active participants formed a very small minority of the soldiers concerned. On both occasions the attitude of the majority was, for a time at any rate, passive. While, finally, in both cases there arose a general feeling of resentment at the excesses which inevitably occurred. These points of similarity disclose, on the part of the men concerned, a profound misapprehension of their duty under such circumstances.
> Apparently on both occasions the majority considered themselves innocent spectators. There can be no innocent spectators at a mutiny. Every soldier present is involved either as a supporter or opponent. There can be no neutrals at a mutiny. The man who, out of curiosity hangs about the outskirts of the crowd, is in the eyes of the law almost as seriously involved as the actual ringleader. In such a situation there are only two courses open to you. Either to remain in your huts, unless otherwise ordered, or to assist to your utmost in quelling the disturbance. It must be clearly understood that, in the presence of mutiny, inaction is almost as serious a crime as active participation.
> From now on, therefore, everyone of you must assist actively and promptly in suppressing the spirit of disorder wherever it manifests itself. Collective disorder must be stamped out — it must never be allowed to gain headway. The man who being present at a mutiny fails to assist in suppressing it will find himself in the same class as the ringleader, who is a criminal and a disgrace to Canada. He will be subject to the same treatment during the disturbance and will be liable to severe penalty afterwards. There can be no possibility of further misunderstanding.
> An impression seems to be abroad that there will be a general amnesty for Military offenders upon a declaration of Peace. So far as I know this is a totally false impression. The Canadian Military Authorities will show no leniency to men who have been found guilty of offences of a mutinous nature: indeed a special record of such cases is being maintained.
> It is now only a matter of weeks before the force of which we are all so proud will have ceased to exist. We who are left in this Country form only the remnants, but in our hands its reputation lies. In the few weeks which yet remain that reputation may suffer irreparable harm.
> I am not going to remind you of our record in France. That point has been amply explained before — apparently without avail. I am not going to remind you of the distress which these outbreaks cause our families and friends at home. Of that you yourself must be fully aware. What I have to say to you now is that these disturbances must and shall cease and that whatever steps are necessary will be taken to ensure that they do cease.
>
> R. W. Turner,
> Lieutenant-General.

## Open questions / uncertain items (June 1919)
- 7 June: "Lieut. Keys placed in arrest" — no reason is given in the diary.
- 13 June: the line "60 O.R. for return to Canada with dependents proceeded to Buxton" is handwritten, added to the typed sheet.
- 16 June: "Muster parade." is handwritten on the typed sheet.
- 17 June: "Damage to private property done by troops at night" — no further detail; special police were put on duty at night from 18 June.
- 18 June: "Lt. Gen. R.W.E. Turner V.C. visited camp and addressed troops." The diary gives his initials as "R.W.E."; his Special Order of the Day (Appendix p. 11) is signed "R. W. Turner" and headed "Lieut-General Sir R.W. Turner V.C. K.C.B. K.C.M.G. D.S.O." — the "E" in the diary entry is as written and may be an error in the original.
- 24 June: "Trial by G.C.M. of Lieut. A.C. McKee" — no charge or outcome is given.
- 27 June: the destination to which the 14 Other Ranks for return by "Olympic" proceeded could not be read; marked [uncertain].
- 28 June: "Peace Treaty signed" — the Treaty of Versailles was signed on 28 June 1919.
- 30 June: the word after "11" in "Final party 11 Orders" appears to read "Orders" but may be "Officers" or "O.Rs"; marked [uncertain]. This is the diary's closing entry: "3rd C.E.R.B. ceases to exist as a Unit in England."
- Appendix pp. 5–8 (Movement Order 22-D-516): the last line of the addressee list reads "S.M.O. [uncertain]" — a word or initials after "S.M.O." could not be read. Party No. 4's dispersal list includes "T1 (Revelstoke)" as written. The cadet surname on Sheet 3 reads "Dougins" but may be "Douglas"; marked [uncertain].
- Appendix pp. 9–10 (Sailing No. 71, S.S. "Belgic"): the sheets are a very faint carbon copy with heavy show-through. The faint parenthetical after "TRAIN NO. 4. (Dispersal Area "E" & "M")" could not be read; the word before "Area "E"" in "Lieut. J.A. Dorion ([uncertain] Area "E")" could not be read (it may be "i/c"). Section 6 ends mid-sentence at the foot of Sheet 1 and is not completed on Sheet 2. The copies list on Sheet 2 gives "Lieut. R.F. Christie" and "Lieut. E.J. Draper" and "Lieut. R.C. Matthews", while Sheet 1 gives "Lieut. H.F. Christie", "Lieut. J.J. Draper" and "Lieut. R.C. Mathews" — the discrepancies are in the original and are transcribed as written on each sheet. One line in the copies list reads "Lieut. [uncertain] J.A. McMillan." — a word before the name could not be read.
- No dates are missing: every day 1–30 June 1919 has an entry. No deaths, casualties or hospital admissions are recorded in June 1919. No map coordinates (latitude/longitude) are given; the only places named are Seaford, Buxton, Southampton, Liverpool, France (War Graves Commission, in May) and the dispersal areas listed in the movement orders.

- Demobilisation totals in June 1919 (as recorded): 14 June — 6 officers and 304 O.R's left Seaford for Southampton to sail on Aquitania with the 18th Res. Bn and 1st B.C. Res. Bn; 23 June — the battalion sailing, 15 officers and 725 O.R's to Liverpool for S.S. Belgic (train No. 4: 121 O.R's; train No. 5: 605 O.R's — 726 in all, one more than the 725 given in the diary entry); 25 June — Capt. JC Brown to Liverpool for S.S. Baltic; 27 June — 14 O.R's for S.S. Olympic. The diary's last entry, 30 June 1919, closes the battalion in England.
