> The following report of the work done in the Dental Department of this Unit, has been submitted to me by Capt. C.H.A.Stevenson, the Officer in charge of that Department:—
> Our work here is primarily for patients in our own Hospital, but besides this, we have done a considerable amount for the men of neighbouring Units, as well as Officers and Nursing Sisters of our own and other Units. For the patients, the chief work has consisted in extractions and prosthesis. The great proportion of patients admitted to Hospital should have extensive extractions made, and prosthetic appliances supplied. The limited equipment and staff, however, render it impossible to do more than the most pressing work of this nature, but despite these limitations, a great deal of prosthetic work has been done with very good results. A large number of extractions have been made, most of which have been made under local anaesthesia, but a few of the more extensive cases have been sent to the Operating Room for general anaesthesia. Our filling operations are limited to the simpler plastic materials, such as cement, amalgam, etc. We have done a considerable amount of work along this line for our own Officers and Nursing Sisters, as well as for the men of our own and other Hospitals. We have a very good operating equipment for the class of work we are able to do, and both Captain Thornton and myself have a complete operating outfit. Our laboratory is fairly well equipped, and is probably as good as it is possible to have it under existing conditions, (i.e., under canvas, with the necessary lack of light, gas jets, running water, and heating appliances, etc.) so that we are unable to work as quickly as under more favourable circumstances; but in spite of these difficulties, we have done a considerable amount of very good mechanical work. The following brief summary may be of interest:— From 19th July to 31st October — Total number of patients admitted to Dental Department — 350. From No. Gill Unit and Hospital — 225. From all other Units — 125. Total number of fillings — 220. Total number of extractions — 150. Total number of prophylaxis —75. Total number of prosthetic — 20.
> GENERAL OBSERVATIONS. Speaking in general terms, the oral condition of the patients of the patients admitted from combatant units, was very bad. In some instances, there seems to have been lacking, any attempt to correct dental deficiencies on enlistment. When one considers the absence of facilities for cleansing the teeth at the front, the frightful result after a few months, may well be imagined. Several cases have come under our notice, where some or all the teeth have been loosened; this, the patients said, was caused by the extreme detonation of a shrapnel shell, or a bomb bursting in the vicinity. These cases, from a civil viewpoint, are among the most interesting. Many cases suffering from Gastritis, have been referred to this Department. > With reference to the work done in the X-Ray Department of this Unit, the following is a report submitted to me by Captains Pirie and Wilkins, Officers i/c of that Department:—
> INTRODUCTION. The first work done in connection with the X-Ray Department was a visit to New York, where a type of apparatus was arranged for, and seen being built. Two such apparati were ordered and transferred to Ottawa, where we unpacked, tested, and repacked them. They arrived in Camiers in good condition.
> PREPARATORY AT CAMIERS. Previous to the opening of the Hospital, the X-Ray Staff were kept busy making preparations for the opening of the Department. The quarters allotted are:— X-Ray Room; Developing Room; Engine Room; Tent. This was found sufficient. A fluoroscopic room would have been an advantage. The X-Ray room was made light tight with blinds specially built for the purpose, so that the room could be quickly lightened and darkened. An X-Ray table was built from a standard table as used in the Wards, with an X-Ray proof tube box under the table. The wires carrying the current, were placed in an underground tunnel, and led through the floor to the X-Ray tube under the table, thus avoiding any chance of shock to the operator or spectators. This has proved satisfactory in every way. An upright fluoroscope was also built, and has proved equally satisfactory.
> APPARATUS. Two X-Ray machines of the type known as "Interruptless Transformers", were supplied to the Department. Each machine consisted of a 10 H.P. Petrol Engine of the "Marine" type, a 2 K.W. Generator, and a 2 K.W. Transformer, supplied by Messrs. Waite and Bartlett of New York. The two machines were similar, with parts interchangeable, so that if one went wrong while the other was out of order, one could be repaired by borrowing from the other machine. Owing to this, one apparatus has always been in working order. Each machine can be taken apart and packed in four boxes for transport. Four men can carry the heaviest box (480 lbs.).

All the tubes were 7" in diameter, with solid Tungsten Targets. All accessories such as plates, paper, lamps, aprons, gloves, chemicals, etc., arrived undamaged, and nothing was lacking in equipment.
> STAFF. The Staff consisted of Captains Pirie and Wilkins, Privates Mathewson, Simpkins and Nicholl; the latter was later replaced by Private Malcolm. At times, an extra orderly was required to carry patients, when the Department was very busy after the arrival of a convoy.
> OPENING OF THE DEPARTMENT. On August 8th, 1915, the Department opened, and 14 radiographs were made. On the third day, 85 were taken, and many fluoroscopic examinations were made, as well as localisations of foreign bodies. On this occasion, one machine was at work from 6.30 a.m. to 7.00 p.m. and broke down. The armature burned out owing to too constant and prolonged use. No precautions had been taken to save it, the requirements of the Patients alone being considered, since then, the other apparatus has been in daily use, and precautions have been taken that it is never overtaxed. It is still in perfect order.
> ROUTINE. All requisitions are attended to the same day as required, but on three occasions, some of the less urgent cases have been left over till the following day, owing to the pressure of work. This delay has been caused by:— (1) The lack of the second apparatus; (2) The length of time required to move a seriously wounded helpless patient; (3) Heavy rain; (4) Darkness, rendering the transport of patients difficult and dangerous after 5.30 p.m. At no time has a patient suffered from want of X-Ray attention.
> RADIOGRAPHS. By the use of paper, instead of plates, we have, as occasion required, been able to show a Surgeon an X-Ray photograph of his case one minute after the taking of the photograph. As a general rule, all radiographs are developed at once, and ready for inspection half an hour later. During rush times, it has been found more practical to show to the Surgeons their radiographs during the evening after the work of the Department is finished. This avoids stopping the X-Ray work during the day in order to consult with the Surgeons, and is a great saving of time to the Department. From August 8th to November 5th, 1915, 1540 X-Ray photographs and many fluoroscopic examinations were made. X-Ray treatments have been given for Synosis [uncertain — as typed] and Rodent ulcer. No. X-Ray burn, or other untoward effect has been caused to patients or Staff. Stomach examinations by Barium meals have been carried out when required. The nature of the findings has been approximately in the following proportions:— Foreign Bodies — 60%. Negative diagnosis — 30%. Fractures — 5%. Diseases — 5%.

It is a pleasure to record the cordial support and co-operation which we have received at all times from all Departments, and the efficient and liberal manner in which we have been supplied with all necessities.

> [Sanitary Department report, continued:] a very good one from many points of view. It was close to a railway, vegetation was not too luxuriant, and it seemed fairly dry, with sufficient slope for efficient drainage. The absence of trees on all sides left the camp very much exposed to high winds and rain — especially if it were intended as a permanent tented Hospital. The light was splendid, and the water supply had been assured, (although later events disproved this latter statement).
> FORMATION OF SOIL. The geological formation consisted of about three feet of sandy friable soil lying over a deep strata of disintegrated chalk. Between the chalk and the green sand underlying it, occurs a thick stratum of clay which forms a seal for any water supply drawn from the sand. It may be stated that several other Units had preceded us — two other Hospitals — a Company of Royal Engineers, and Base Details.
> WATER SUPPLY. The first problem to be dealt with was the water supply. The supply came from an artesian well, sunk about 84 metres, tapping the green sand as indicated before. The water, though somewhat hard, was of great purity; it was made available for use at two (stand) pipes, one within our lines, and within the lines occupied by a neighbouring Unit. On inspection, it was found that these water pipes passed through soil that had received a considerable degree of pollution from soak pits made by Royal Engineers and Base Details. In one particular instance, the water pipe was discovered passing through a pit, in which excremental matter had been buried, and as the joint at that spot was defective, there was some danger of water contamination. To prevent such, all drinking water was passed through the Army Regulation Water Carts, two of which had been supplied to our Unit. These water carts had clarifying and filtering apparatus attached, but to be doubly sure, the water was also chlorinated. This was continued for a period of two months when the water supply was laid on, and conditions mentioned above removed. During the first two days, it was discovered that a few men were in the habit of occasionally drawing water from a shallow well in the yard of a Peasant, about one kilometre distant. These men had been assured that this well contained splendid drinking water. On examination, it was found to be beautiful and sparkling, but it had received a great deal of sewage, as the sub-soil drain from the farm-yard opened directly into the well about two feet from the top. This practice was of course immediately prohibited, and the well was shortly afterwards closed to all troops. It may at once be said that the idea of a water carriage system was not, and has not yet, been approved by the War Office. This is rather unfortunate, as the practicability of introducing land irrigation, or filtration through coke, can be easily established. I am still of the opinion, (as expressed in an early report) that on account of liability of chalk and clay to fissure and crack, there is the possibility, remote perhaps, of the water supply becoming polluted at some future date.

> [Sanitary Department report, continued:] DISPOSAL OF EXCRETA. Latrines had been constructed, provided with pails, and were ready for use the day after arrival. A large "Horsefall" Destructor was also available in which the contents of the pails were destroyed. These Destructors, two of which were provided for our Unit, were placed in huts on a concrete floor, with sufficient roof and sides to afford protection from the rains. In the huts also, there are two brick-bins, one to contain sawdust, and the other kindlings and materials to start the fires. The excreta from pails is brought to the Destructor where it is mixed with sawdust and some dry refuse and then put in the Destructor, and quickly burned. The Destructors, once started, rarely require any fuel other than the Camp refuse. Very occasionally it may be necessary to add a small quantity of coal. It is remarkable how effective these Destructors have proved. All the refuse from the latrines, mess-rooms, wards, and kitchens is readily and quickly destroyed, leaving behind some ashes, clinkers and tins. The ashes and clinkers were used for paths and roads, and the tins, when perforated, for filling up pits. > DISPOSAL OF SULLAGE WATER. Disposal of all waste waters from cook houses, ablution tables and baths, was and always will, remain a difficult problem on account of the indifferent porosity of the soil. All these liquids are heavily charged with greasy matter such as soap, small parts of bread, meat, vegetables, refuse, and fats from cook-houses, and blood and other substances from Hospital buildings. Before these liquids could be gotten rid of into the earth, the grease had to be removed by some form of sedimentation or filtration, as the accumulation of grease and soapy material, would soon render any soil ineffective. The very simplest arrangements were resorted to for the first week, viz., small perforated biscuit-tins which were filled with straw and covered with a sack, and then placed in a small pit in the ground. At the end of that time permanent grease traps were introduced. In the ablution huts, soapy waters were first received into large boxes of a capacity of two cubic feet and filled with coke; the bottoms of these boxes were perforated, and the liquids led into pipes which conveyed them into a series of pits. A system which might be described as a "tandem" system was adopted. It consisted in allowing sullage water to run into a pit of varying depth and width in which some coke was placed, with a baffle-board arranged one foot from the discharge end of pit, and 6" from the bottom. From this pit, connections were made by means of "siphons" with two similar pits so that any overflow from the first could be directed into one or other of the accessory pits. Simple "shut-offs" were placed in siphons in order that each pit could be rested when necessary, thus allowing plenty of time for the water to soak away.

As is to be expected Haemothorax is very common; by the time patients are in our hands, Pneumothorax is quite rare. It is likely that most of these cases have a small amount of air free in the pleura at first, which air is quickly absorbed. We have adopted conservatism in the matter of evacuating such patients, keeping them until two or three weeks have elapsed to prevent any untoward accidents in moving. We have further adopted Colonel Sir John Rose Bradford's advice, and where possible, after the tenth day, have aspirated the chest, removing as much blood as possible and replacing it by oxygen, with the view to prevent further hemorrhage which might follow a lowering of the pressure in the pleural space. On more than one occasion anaerobic bacilli have been found in the blood removed. Contrary to what one might expect, the blood is usually fluid and is removed with difficulty; frequently one finds difficulty in piercing the large layer of fibrin in the pleural space.
