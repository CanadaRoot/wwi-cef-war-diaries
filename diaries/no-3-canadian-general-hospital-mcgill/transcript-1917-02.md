Haemo-thorax is usually demonstrable, and the history of hemoptysis frequently is present. The rapidity with which blood is spat up after wounding, depends upon whether the upper or lower part of the lung is wounded, the haemorrhage being most prompt in the case of the former. Fever is usually present in the earlier days, often disappearing by the 6th or 7th day. When the fever continues, we draw up blood from the pleura for the purpose of culture; such cultures usually prove sterile. As a usual thing, they draw off by aspiration the blood, about the 10th day; sometimes the blood so drawn off is replaced in a few days by effusion, so that thorough aspirations can be made. The blood so drawn off is sometimes replaced by oxygen, the outflow of blood, and the inflow of oxygen being made through separate needles at the same time. Of late, we have not felt so keenly the necessity of the use of oxygen; our idea was that replacement allowed a less chance of disturbance of the bulk of the lung with consequent haemorrhage. An incomplicated case is fit to travel, we judge, about the 13th or 14th day. The possibility of sea-sickness and vomiting is the chief reason against allowing cases to travel at an earlier date, as the repair of the lung wound seems to be relatively slow. With shell wounds of the lung, and in cases where the foreign body remains in the chest cavity, no set method of treatment is possible. The X-Rays and the fluoroscopic screens are used to the fullest extent in these cases; a certain small per cent of cases with foreign body are found amenable to early operation, with removal. The relative infrequency of infection of the pleural blood is remarkable. In the hospital under my command, only two cases of gas infection of the pleural blood have been seen, both of which recovered. A number of other cases by large bacilli, which might have been gas bacilli, but were not certainly so, have been treated as empyema and drained, and so far as we know, with recovery in all cases.

> 5. Considering the number of cases of continued fever coming to the hospital under my command, it becomes necessary to divide them into the constituent diseases, viz., typhoid and para-typhoid fevers, so called trench fever, and other less specific infections. Since the whole Army is inoculated, the clinician is no longer able to determine whether a case of continued fever be typhoid, para-typhoid, or another. The classical signs of enlarged palpable spleen, rose spots, are too often absent. A dirty tongue implies gastro-intestinal disturbance, but it is in no wise specific. The old fashioned Widal test is useless because it is positive by reason of inoculation. It therefore becomes necessary to make a quantitative agglutination test, which is in series. This is done at intervals of not less than four days, and the positive diagnosis of typhoid, or para-typhoid "A" or "B" may be possible by observing that there is an agglutination curve, and not a stable agglutination line. From this, it will be inferred that a diagnosis is made by a quantitative fall in agglutination power, as well as by a rise, the fall or rise depending upon the phase in agglutination power, which the blood shows at the time it is taken. No longer is the typical typhoid temperature chart to be seen. Regular, more or less continued fever, or even an acutely relapsing fever is to be seen. > 6. I wish to place on record some very interesting and important facts regarding the methods of localizing foreign bodies. A large percentage of the work done in an operating room in war time at a Base Hospital such as this, consists in the removal of foreign bodies. It is essential that this should be done with the least possible amount of traumatism, and this means that the position of the foreign body should be definitely known before the operator begins to work. The localization of these foreign bodies becomes, therefore, almost an art in itself, and the development of that art (if one may so call it) in the course of this war, would be perfectly amazing to a civil surgeon. Hereunder, therefore, will be found a brief account of the different methods used to locate accurately any foreign body in the wounded soldiers who come to the hospital under my command, which have been noticed by Lt.Colonel Elder and myself.
>
> (a) Captain A.Howard Pirie, CAMC, my Radiographical expert has devised a very ingenious scale, based upon the fact that the shadow of the foreign body increases in size as the plate is removed farther from the body. I am speaking of foreign bodies of known dimensions, e.g., rifle bullets, and shrapnel balls. Suppose, then, the plate to be in contact with the skin, all one has to do, is to measure the size of the skiagram of the foreign body, and compare it with the scale, which will at once indicate the depth from the skin surface, of the foreign body. A reference to the cross section atlas will then at once give one the position of the foreign body.

> (d) As aids in the operating room, we place, first, the large Electro-Magnet, bearing the name of Bergonie of Paris. By its use, we can determine the exact position of all electro-magnetic substances, and fortunately, German bullets are electro-magnetic, whilst English and French bullets are not. Even when these bodies are deeply situated, and their vibration cannot readily be made out by the hand, it can easily be heard by the stethoscope, placed on the skin, opposite the electro-magnet. A sound, very like a steamboat whistle, indicates the nearest point to the foreign body, and the skin is marked at that point. In the case of non-magnetic foreign bodies, such as lead, brass, and nickel, or in the case where a magnetic foreign body is embedded in bone, and therefore cannot vibrate, we have recourse to the use of the telephone probe, or rather, forceps. This is of great assistance in locating the [uncertain — struck-through word, illegible] foreign body, either in the soft tissues, or in the bone, and enables us to extract it, with a minimum of damage to the tissues. In other cases, again, when the foreign body will not vibrate, or has possibly changed its position in the soft parts since the X-Ray picture was taken (and this is notably so with foreign bodies in joints) we operate under the fluoroscopic screen, and are thus able to go directly to the foreign body, and remove it.

> REPORT ON THE SURGICAL DEPARTMENT OF No.3.CANADIAN GENERAL HOSPITAL (McGill)
>
> By:- Lt.Colonel J.M.Elder,CAMC.
>
> Officer i/c Surgery.
>
> This report covers the work of the hospital from its inception, up to January 31st 1917:-
>
> (1) STATISTICS
>
> Total number of patients admitted to the hospital, 41,576. Of these, 19,269 were classified as "wounded", the remainder, as "sick". The total number of deaths which have occurred in the hospital was 197, the mortality being less than half of one per cent (0.47%). 4,349 operations have been done in the Operating Theatre, with a total post-operative mortality of 2.5%.
>
> (2) TREATMENT OF SEPTIC WOUNDS.
>
> As most of the wounds we have to treat come under this category, we have depended very largely on free opening and drainage of these wounds. We make our own drainage tubes, using oil silk tissue, as we very early realized the danger of ordinary rubber tubing, inasmuch as its use predisposed to secondary haemorrhage in limbs, and faecal fistulae in abdominal cases. In both instances, a pressure necrosis takes place. Eusol has been our chief antiseptic solution.
>
> Carrel's tubes, as they come down to us, have been far from satisfactory; often because they are improperly placed in the wound, but chiefly because they could not be often enough irrigated during the transit of the patients to us. They are only of value if placed in a wound cavity, and frequently irrigated; they are not drains. We use them frequently here to clean up suppurating cavities, which can be partially closed, but as soon as these cavities become clean, our preference then is to treat them with Mr.Rutherford Morison's bismuth and iodoform paste; these cavities can then be closed, and will only need dressing about every third day, - a great saving of time here, and an immense advantage when it is necessary to evacuate these patients to England.
>
> (3) SEPTIC KNEE-JOINTS.
>
> Our routine procedure in these cases is to keep the joint cavity closed, if possible, and control the sepsis by repeated aspirations of the joint. After each aspiration, a small quantity of two per cent formalin in glycerin is injected into the joint. This aseptic irritant produces a leucocytosis, and this enables the synovial membrane to combat the sepsis. Our success has been most gratifying, and the majority of the septic joints, so treated, have quite recovered their function.

> (4) TRENCH FOOT AND FROST BITE.
>
> Last year, we had very many cases of Trench Foot. This winter, we have had mainly to deal with Frost-bite. With our experience in Canada to guide us, we are easily able to differentiate between the two conditions. In Trench Foot, there is always infection present, and therefore systemic disturbances, and the constant danger of moist gangrene. In Frost-bite, on the contrary, infection is an accident, and is rarely present, and the tendency is to dry gangrene. Trench Foot requires often moist antiseptic dressings, and always very careful watching as to extension of the septic process up the limb. Frost-bite should, if possible, be kept dry, and done up in cotton wool, with boracic powder. The gangrene tends to form a definite line of demarcation, and not to spread up the leg. In neither condition have we had Tetanus infection, though in all cases where the skin was broken, we have followed the instructions as to giving a prophylactic dose of the serum. One would suppose that there would be much more danger of Tetanus in Trench Foot than frost bite. During twentyfive years of experience in a Civil Hospital in Canada, I never saw Tetanus as a complication of Frost-bite.
>

> LOCALIZATION OF BULLETS AND SHRAPNEL BALLS BY ONE RADIOGRAPH ON ONE PLATE.

>
> Many bullets and shrapnel balls are found quite undeformed in the wounded, and it is then possible to make a rapid localization of them from a single radiograph on a single plate. It is assumed that such bullets and shrapnel balls are of constant shape and size. This we assume from practical experience during the last 14 months. To carry out the method it is necessary to have a key radiograph (Fig. 1). This was made by making radiographs of a bullet and shrapnel ball at the following distances from the plate, 0, ½, 1, 1½, 2, 2½, 3, 3½, 4, 4½, 5, 5½, and 6 inches. As Fig. 1 is reproduced natural size, it will act as a key radiograph to those who wish to use the method.
>
> Bullets.—The diameter of a bullet casts a shadow which is proportional to its distance from the plate, no matter at what angle the bullet is lying. The length of the bullet is no guide as to its depth, but under all circumstances its

It has a uniformly cylindrical part, which at one end tapers to a point and at the other end is blunt and indented. It is the uniformly cylindrical part which acts as our guide, and, by measuring its greatest diameter, then comparing this measurement with the corresponding key figure, we get the distance from the plate of that part of the bullet measured. The bullet should theoretically be in the perpendicular ray, but for practical purposes, if it is within six inches the error is small and may be neglected. With a fine pair of dividers the greatest diameter of the shadow is measured and the dividers then transferred to the key plate and there placed on the bullet shadow which they fit exactly. The distance of the bullet under consideration from the plate is then the same as the known distance of the bullet in the key plate. > The Degree of Blurring of the Shadow.—On many occasions during a rush time, when we have made as many as 89 radiographs in a day, when only one radiograph had been made of a patient, we have been able to form an estimate of the depth of a piece of shrapnel or bomb by considering the degree of blurring of its shadow.

At the same time one can estimate how still the patient has kept by the blurring or sharpness of one of his bones near the shrapnel. When the FB is in the chest or abdomen no reliance can be placed on the degree of blurring. One would not advocate serious localization by such a method, but during rush times it has proved of practical value.

>
> (1) Bullets and shrapnel balls of the present war can be localized by a single radiograph.

>

>
> (4) The Hughes balance in a modified form is useful for locating foreign bodies.
