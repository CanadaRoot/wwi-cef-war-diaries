## Transcription conventions
- Faithful to the original: spelling, punctuation, abbreviations and capitalisation as written. Typewritten on Army Form C. 2118, "WAR DIARY or INTELLIGENCE SUMMARY", in columns: Place | Date | Hour | Summary of Events and Information | Remarks and references to Appendices.
- `[uncertain]` marks a word or passage that could not be read with confidence — never guessed. Only one `[uncertain]` mark was needed this month, on the Agenda table (May 1917, p. 7), where a figure before "1.63%" is struck through and cannot be read; see the open questions below.
- As in March, on the diary-text pages the sub-heading "INTELLIGENCE SUMMARY" IS struck through, with "or" handwritten above it — the heading reads "WAR DIARY or INTELLIGENCE SUMMARY", with "(Erase heading not required.)" beneath it.
- The Remarks column throughout is the handwritten initials "H.S.B." (Colonel H. S. Birkett), once per dated row; the long 4 May block carries additional sets of initials beside its sub-items. No initials appear in the Remarks column on the first Agenda page (May 1917, p. 6).
- The printed footer on all diary-text pages reads "2449 Wt. W14957/M90 750,000 1/16 J.B.C. & A. Forms/C.2118/12." (the same print run as March) and the form carries the printed instructions block at top left ("Instructions regarding War Diaries and Intelligence Summaries are contained in F. S. - The oval Office of Officer Commanding stamp ("OFFICE OF OFFICER COMMANDING / 1 - JUN. 1917 / No. 2 WD. 5-17 / No. 3 Canadian General Hospital—McGill") appears on the cover and on all diary-text pages.
- No hours are recorded in May — the Hour column is blank throughout.
- Unlike March, no admission or discharge figures are given in the diary text this month at all — the diary is kept as narrative entries, and fifteen days are recorded as "Nothing of any moment". The only monthly patient totals are those in the Agenda table on May 1917, p. 7: May 1917, total admissions 4,570, deaths 39 (0.86%); total operations 389, post-operative deaths 19 (4.88%). No discharge total for May is stated anywhere in the volume.
- Unlike March, the Place column carries no ditto marks: "Boulogne" is typed only on the first row of diary-text pages 1 and 2 (1 May and 10 May), and the Place column is left blank for all other rows, including the whole of diary-text page 3 (24–31 May). Blank Place cells in the transcription below reproduce that blank.
- Ditto marks (") in the Date column are not used; every day 1–31 May has its number entered. Ditto rows under "Nothing of any moment" are transcribed as " " " "............ as typed.
- The 10 May entry begins at the foot of diary-text page 1 and continues at the top of diary-text page 2; it is transcribed as two rows, one per page, as written.
- The word "Capt" opening the 17 May entry is typed at the end of the 16 May line; the date "17" is placed beside the following line. The Enwright entry is therefore transcribed under 17 May; see the open questions below.

> | Place | Date | Hour | Summary of Events and Information | Remarks |
> | | 1917 May 24 | | Capt. W.J. Enwright ceases to be attached on proceeding to No.8. Canadian General Hospital............Thirty-three N.C.O's and men returned to No.57 General Hospital for duty, the work that they have done here being very much appreciated............The attention of the Sanitary authorities has been directed to the quality of what is known as "French Milk", i.e., the milk supplied to this hospital from local sources. During the week, a whole delivery has been condemned as being sour and quite unfit for use. The milk was sour on arrival, and when an attempt was made to boil it, it curdled. A shipment of English milk has been inspected, but the contents were anything but clean. Dust, bran, and even straw in abundance has been seen floating on the top of the milk on arrival, and the covers of the cans have been found to be ill fitting, i.e., loose, leaving a space where any dust, or infection, may blow in. It is quite true that this milk is to be boiled, but I see no reason why it should not be handled in a more cleanly fashion, considering the journey, and the approach of the warmer weather. Unless these conditions can be improved, it is quite evident that this milk will be sour upon reaching this hospital. The Cesspit at the rear of the Operating Room is very nearly full again, and very shortly will want emptying. It is at present about one foot from the top of the crown although it is only a month ago that this was emptied. It seems unfortunate that this service cannot be connected with the main drain, which runs alongside the tank at a higher level. It would be a comparatively simple matter to connect the drain from the ward, just outside the building with the main drain, thus doing away with the tank altogether. I have recommended that this be done. The attempt at a soakage pit which was constructed at the new latrine has proven a failure. At present, it looks as though a simple trench was filled with stones, which has now overflowed, and is causing unpleasantness. This has been pointed out to competent authority here, with recommendation for a drain, which would carry the urine from a properly constructed urinal, instead of a bucket system which obtains at present. The whole drain would be about 300 feet. | H.S.B. |

> (1) Major J.W. Hutchinson, CAMC, of the Unit under my command, has given special attention to Gunshot wounds of the chest, and the following are some very interesting notes which he has written thereon:- "During the past six weeks, we have treated 146 cases of penetrating wounds of the chest in the Ward specially set aside for such cases. Of these, 144 had blood in the pleural cavity, and two had serum. Infection occurred in 31 of these cases; the others remained uncomplicated. Of the 31 infected cases, as proved by staining and cultural properties 20 were due to the streptococcus, 9 to the bacillus aerogenes capsulatus, and 2 to the pneumococcus. Twentyseven of the infected cases were operated on, of which eight died. The others were evacuated to England in good condition, or are still under treatment. Three of the non-operated cases died, and one, a case of pneumococcus infection, is under treatment by aspiration irrigation with Eusol, and is doing well. Of the twenty cases infected with the streptococcus, eleven were uncomplicated and did well. The other nine died from the following causes:- Three from abscess of the lung, one from septicaemia, one from multiple wounds and purulent pericarditis; three from pneumonia of the opposite side; one was almost moribund when seen, as the result of an open Pyo-Pneumo-thorax. Of the nine cases infected with the bacillus aerogenes capsulates, eight were operated on, and one was in such bad condition that operation was out of the question. Of the two cases infected with the pneumococcus, one was operated on, and one is under treatment by aspiration and irrigation with Eusol. The operations performed in the first twenty-two cases were those of resection of a rib, thorough cleaning out of all blood and clot from the pleural cavity. While working on these cases, it occurred to me that much better results could be obtained if the chest could be kept closed. I therefore tried by repeated aspirations and irrigations with Eusol ( 1 in 2) to sterilize the infected Haemothorax. The first case was one infected with the bacillus aerogenes capsulates, which was discovered very early before serious symptoms had developed. Under aspiration and irrigation with Eusol I managed to control the condition for a few days, but at last I found it very difficult to aspirate the fluid from the chest on account of the presence of blood clot. I then opened the chest, cleaned out the blood and clot and closed the pleural cavity. During the next two weeks, I aspirated and irrigated the chest four times, and now the patient is practically well. Since then, I have performed the same operation on three other cases infected with the bacillus aerogenes capsulates; one case is practically cured; one is doing well; and one had to be re-opened and drained. The same procedure was tried in a case of streptococcus infection, but drainage had to be substituted. > (1). Every case in which a haemo-thorax can be demonstrated clinically, should be aspirated as early as possible after the 5th day, and as much fluid withdrawn.
>
> (2). An early diagnosis of infection of the haemo-thorax is important, and should be suspected when the temperature, pulse, and respirations begin rise.
>
> (3). As soon as there is the least suspicion that infection may have occurred, fluid should be withdrawn, and a bacteriological examination made. If the first examination is negative, and there is no amelioration of the symptoms, another puncture should be done.
>
> (4). Operation should be performed as soon as infection has been proved, with a thorough cleaning out of all blood and clot.
>
> (5). Pneumococcal infections of the fluid, when there is comparatively little clot, may be treated by aspiration and irrigation.
>
> (6). Cases infected with a pure culture of the bacillus aerogenes capsulates may be opened, completely cleaned of all blood and clot, irrigated and closed. This should be followed by occasional aspiration and irrigation.
>
> (7). Cases of streptococcal infection should be opened, cleaned of blood and clot, and drained.
