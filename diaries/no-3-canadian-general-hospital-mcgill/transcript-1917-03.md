## Transcription conventions
- Faithful to the original: spelling, punctuation, abbreviations and capitalisation as written. Typewritten on Army Form C. 2118, "WAR DIARY or INTELLIGENCE SUMMARY", in columns: Place | Date | Hour | Summary of Events and Information | Remarks and references to Appendices.
- `[uncertain]` marks a word or passage that could not be read with confidence — never guessed. Two `[uncertain]` marks were needed this month, both on the photograph sheet (March 1917, p. 7), whose text is too faint to read; see the open questions below.
- Unlike December, on the March diary-text pages the sub-heading "INTELLIGENCE SUMMARY" IS struck through, with "or" handwritten above it — the heading reads "WAR DIARY or INTELLIGENCE SUMMARY", with "(Erase heading not required.)" beneath it.
- The Remarks column throughout is the handwritten initials "H.S.B." (Colonel H. S. Birkett).
- The printed footer on all diary-text pages reads "2449 Wt. W14957/M90 750,000 1/16 J.B.C. & A. Forms/C.2118/12." (a different print run from December's "2353 Wt. W2544/1454 700,000 5/15 D. D. & L. A.D.S.S. Forms/C 2118.") and the form carries the printed instructions block at top left ("Instructions regarding War Diaries and Intelligence Summaries are contained in F. S. Regs., Part II. and the Staff Manual respectively. Title pages will be prepared in manuscript.").
- The oval Office of Officer Commanding stamp ("OFFICE OF OFFICER COMMANDING / 31 MAR. 1917 / No. 2 WD. 3-17 / No. 3 Canadian General Hospital—McGill") appears on the cover and on all diary-text pages.
- No hours are recorded in March — the Hour column is blank throughout. The diary is kept as daily admission and discharge totals; the word used for patients leaving is "discharged" throughout (no "evacuated" figures this month — "evacuated" is used only for individuals sent to England).
- As in December, ditto marks (") in the Place and Date columns are expanded to the repeated value in the transcription below; on page two a dash in the Place column (16 March) is likewise expanded to Boulogne.
- 28 March has no admission or discharge figures. 31 March has no admission or discharge figures and no date entered in the Date column; the date "31" is supplied from the sequence and from "Boulogne. 31-3-17" at the foot of the Agenda.
- The Agenda begins mid-entry on 31 March (the word "AGENDA." is typed, underlined, across the line after "kitchens.") and continues over diary-text pages 4–6, with a photograph sheet inserted between pages 4 and 5.

> I wish to draw your attention to the following original suggestions of Major L.J.Rhea, O., i/c of Pathology in the Unit under my command which have been put into practice by him during the past six months, and which have been brought about by war conditions necessitating the strictest economy, and the inability to obtain such material as would have been at his disposal in peace times:-
>
> (1). A MODIFIED TEAT FOR DROPPING PIPETTES.
>
> "The accuracy of the dropping pipette and the comfort and rapidity with which it is used depends in part upon the ease with which it can be manipulated. This is especially true when it is used often, and over comparatively long periods, as in making the dilutions in the agglutinating tests for the enteric group of infections, according to Dryer's method.
>
> The rubber teats provided with the dropping pipettes issued under the direction of the Medical Research Committee soon loose much of their elasticity, and it then becomes difficult to fill the pipettes to a sufficient extent and to completely empty them.
>
> I have made a simple attachment to replace these teats, which obviates these difficulties. The attachment, which consists of a rubber stopper, and a heavy rubber vaccine bottle cap is made as follows:-
>
> Bore a hole through the centre of the long axis of the rubber stopper; this hole should be of such a size as to fit tightly over the end of the pipette. Fit a heavy rubber vaccine bottle cap over the larger end of the rubber stopper, and fasten it in place by means of a string, small wire, or rubber cement. Rubber cement is not suitable when a rubber solution is to be used in the pipette dissolves it. The rubber cap should be adjusted so that the vacuum produced is not greater than that required to fill the pipette. A very fine perforation in the centre of the rubber cap makes it easier to prevent the formation of bubbles in the pipette; this perforation may be made with a red hot needle. A piece of pressure rubber tubing, one inch long, may be used instead of a rubber stopper.
>
> When a pipette fitted with this attachment is held between the fingers and the palm of the hand, the thumb is left free to manipulate the rubber cap by pressing directly down upon it. Pipettes fitted with this attachment have been used in our laboratory for some months,

> (2) THE USE OF THE AUTOCLAVE IN THE PREPARATION OF COAGULATED OX BLOOD SERUM (Loeffler's)
>
> "For some years, I have not used a serum Inspissator in the preparation of coagulated ox blood serum (Loeffler's) to be employed as a routine medium. I have used the Autoclave instead. By this means, large quantities can be made at once, and a great deal of time can be saved, as the medium is coagulated and sterilized simultaneously. The time required to do this is only half an hour, instead of from three to five days.
>
> Coagulated ox blood serum made in the Autoclave is firm, has a smooth surface, a good colour, plenty of water of condensation, and serves as an excellent routine culture medium. It is perfectly satisfactory for growing bacillus diphtheriae.
>
> Unless certain precautions are taken to prevent or counterbalance the escape of steam or air during the process of coagulation and sterilization, good results are not always obtained. If the following method, however, is carried out, good results are assured:-
>
> The tubes of medium are slanted on a frame, or in wire baskets, and put into the Autoclave, preferably after the water in it has been warmed. As soon as the top of the apparatus is fastened on, and heat applied, an air pump such as is used to inflate automobile tyres, is attached to the escaped steam valve, and a sufficient amount of air is pumped in to raise the pressure inside the Autoclave to five or eight pounds. The escape steam valve is then closed, and heat is applied until the pressure registers about five pounds above that necessary for sterilization.
>
> As soon as the Autoclave is cooled down, the tubes are removed, and are ready for use.
>
> Serum may be coagulated in Petri dishes as satisfactorily as in tubes. We have found serum coagulated by these means in Petri dishes very useful in the examination of contacts in diphtheria.
>
> The initial positive pressure produced by the air pump prevents boiling of the medium, before coagulation takes place, and the escape of air from it. If the air pump is not used,
