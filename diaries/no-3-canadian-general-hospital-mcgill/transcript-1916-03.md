## Transcription conventions
- Faithful to the original: spelling, punctuation, abbreviations and capitalisation as written. Typewritten on Army Form C. 2118, "WAR DIARY or INTELLIGENCE SUMMARY", in columns: Place | Date | Hour | Summary of Events and Information | Remarks and references to Appendices.
- `[uncertain]` marks a word or name that could not be read with confidence — never guessed.
- The Remarks column throughout is the handwritten initials "H.S.B." (Colonel H. S. Birkett).
- On all six diary-text pages the sub-heading "INTELLIGENCE SUMMARY" is struck through (the heading reads "WAR DIARY" with "INTELLIGENCE SUMMARY" crossed out); "(Erase heading not required.)" appears beneath it.
- Underlined in the original: "PERIOD NUMBER ONE" and "PERIOD NUMBER TWO" (laboratory report headings), and within the text "per primam", "in situ", and "left" (in "left lung").
- The laboratory report from Captain L. J. Rhea has no date or hour entries of its own; it is embodied in the diary across diary-text pp. 3–6, and the dated entries resume on 26 March on diary-text p. 6.
- March entries read "Nothing worthy of **comment** occurred" (as in November and December).

> | Place | Date | Hour | Summary of Events and Information | Remarks |
> | Boulogne, March, 1916. | | | I desire to embody in this Diary an extremely interesting report from the Pathological Laboratory, which is in charge of Captain L.J.Rhea:— The following report from the Laboratory deals with two periods. The first period extends from the time the tented Hospital in Dannes-Camiers was closed up to the time you took over the command of the Hospital in Boulogne. The second period from the time you took over the Hospital in Boulogne to the present time. In this report, these two periods will be referred to as periods number one, and period number two. PERIOD NUMBER ONE. From the closing of the Tent Hospital in Dannes-Camiers, to the time you took over the Hospital in Boulogne. With the closing of the Hospital in Dannes-Camiers, the routine laboratory work became very light, and dealt only with examination of material from members of your Unit who became ill. The great reduction in the routine laboratory work gave us an opportunity to go over with some system the work done since our arrival in France, a detailed study of which had not been possible during the active days of the Hospital. It was my intention to tabulate the work done on the Enteric group of infections; to make a study of the comparatively large series of bacteriological examinations made from the fluids removed from the chest in gun-shot wounds in this location; to make a similar study of fluid removed from the knee-joints in injuries in and about this location, and to study in detail the post-mortem examinations made, with special reference to the cases with injuries of the head. The necessary work of packing equipment to be moved, and the calling away of Captains McKim and Malone to England for duty, made it impossible to carry out the work undertaken. The greatest amount of time has been spent on a study of injuries of the skull and brain. Each post-mortem, from cases of this series, has been gone over from a purely pathological view point, and, in conjunction with the clinicians, from a clinical view point as well. You are familiar with the general scheme of study, and I have already shown you a series of sketches made to illustrate certain points, and have pointed out to you certain findings with which we have been impressed, especially those that have a direct bearing upon the treatment of head injuries in general. (There is one point to which I wish to refer in some detail; injuries of the brain due directly to projectiles, and those due secondary to projectiles, especially from fragments of skull driven into the brain tissue by the impact of a missile. PERIOD NUMBER TWO. Since you took over the Hospital to the present date. The space and general arrangements for laboratory work in Boulogne is far superior to that at Dannes-Camiers. One hut of two rooms has been set apart for the laboratory work, where both running water and electric light have been installed. We have equipped these rooms with the necessary shelf and desk space. Certain changes in the personnel of the Unit, and the demands made by the particular construction, and the increased capacity of the Hospital in Boulogne, has made it necessary for the laboratory to alter in certain respects the work previously undertaken in it. In order that we might carry out our conception of the first duty of laboratory work in the field, that it should be concerned primarily with the cases in hand, it has been necessary to confine our work to the routine that each day brings. As a result, we have had to curtail, and in some instances give up entirely, certain investigations we had undertaken, and indefinitely postpone projected work. If the opportunity presents itself, we hope to undertake this work at a later date. (The routine work of the laboratory is carried on very much the same as at Dannes-Camiers, though certain things are receiving more careful and a more systematic examination; among them, sputum. The careful and prolonged search for tubercule bacilli in the sputum from practically every case of disease associated with clinical signs and symptoms referrable to the lung, has resulted in our being able to make possible a definite diagnosis of tuberculosis in several suspected cases, and to exclude it, in so far as active service laboratories can exclude it in a good number of cases. When diseases of the lung and bronchi are as common as they have been recently, too much emphasis cannot be put upon careful sputum examination. You are already familiar with the fixed routine through which each specimen of sputum is put, as well as certain special technique that is employed. We have continued in so far as possible the routine bacteriological examination of the blood, urine and stools, and a serological examination of all cases with symptoms suggesting infections with the Enteric Group of bacilli. The number of positive cases have been very few when compared with our findings at Dannes-Camiers. One case, examined after two recent inoculations with the triple vaccine gave a positive agglutination to bacillus typhosus, para-typhosus alpha, and para-typhosus beta, as high as one in a thousand. In such cases, a single agglutination is of little value from the view point of a diagnosis, and since a positive diagnosis by the seriological examination would require a sufficient time to establish a curve, it seems to us that a painstaking and most careful bacteriological examination of the stools, blood and urine, is now more important than ever. This is especially true of the more difficult bacteriological examination of the stools. From time to time, there have occurred cases in the Hospital where the exclusion of, or a positive diagnosis of, syphilis was important. In so far as possible, the blood serum of these patients has been sent to Havre for examination. Largely depending upon the distance and the time consumed in travel, several days often elapsed before a report was returned. In order that the benefits of a "Wassermann" test might be more widely available, and that time be saved, we have undertaken to do this test on a certain number of patients in your Hospital; already we have been of assistance to the clinicians, both by positive and negative findings. Since the laboratory has been open in Boulogne, we have done all the laboratory work for No.1 Convalescent Camp. This work largely consists in the examination of sputum for tubercule bacilli, the examination of sore throats and urethral smears......... I wish to draw attention to the following interesting Surgical cases which have come under the immediate care of two members of my Staff. The first under that of Lt.Colonel J.M.Elder is as follows:— A Private of the 1st Canadian Contingent came down from the front suffering from a bullet lodged in the right lobe of the liver, having traversed the stomach. The stomach wound had been closed at the Casualty Clearing Station, and had healed per primam, but no bullet had been found. The bullet was accurately located by Captain Wilkins. Patient developed symptoms of liver abscess, and was operated upon on March 24th. Through anterior abdominal incision, abscess in the liver was evacuated and bullet found in abscess cavity. Abscess cavity was drained through stab-wound in right loin, and anterior incision closed. Patient made a rapid and good recovery......... The second, which came under the care of Major W.H.P.Hill, is as follows:— A Private of the 2nd Suffolk Regiment had a piece of shrapnel enter posterior part of apex of right lung. The foreign body was located by X-Ray in the lower lobe of left lung, slightly posterior, and opposite 7th rib. On March 16th Major Hill excised rib and sewed pleura together. | H.S.B. |
