> | Place | Date | Hour | Summary of Events and Information | Remarks |
> | Boulogne. | April. | | The following is a report of the work done by the X-Ray Department since its establishment at this Base from 15th February to 24th April, 1916:— When the X-Ray Department was closed at Dannes-Camiers all the apparatus was repacked and stored in a hut. It was evident at the time that the apparatus would absorb an amount of moisture in a tent in winter which would make it useless till it was dried thoroughly again. On arrival at Boulogne, the apparatus was unpacked, and it was found that no X-Ray tubes had been broken. Very little damage had been done to the apparatus during transport. The first consideration was to get it all thoroughly dried. In order to do this a stove was put into each room of the X-Ray Department, and leaks in the roof were repaired. The work of the Department had to begin before everything was quite dry, and several breakdowns occurred on account of the moisture. After three weeks the moisture had been got rid of, and little more trouble from that source occurred. The engines were set up in a shed, in which the Engineers prepared a concrete floor for them. The concrete of the floor is only 4" thick, and this is not enough for the engines. It vibrates while the engines are running. At Camiers, we had 12" of concrete and no vibration. High tension wires were run through the walls of the shed, and holes bored in the glass of the windows of the X-Ray room, and thus the electricity is satisfactorily distributed from the Engine Room to the Department. When an X-Ray photo is taken, the noise of the engine is not troublesome. A pit was made for the exhaust of the engines, and this deadens the noise to a large extent. The switches are so arranged that the operator has a thick close wall between him and the X-Ray tube and so runs no danger from the X-Rays. At Camiers, one of the engines broke down, and the repaired part – the armature – arrived after the Department was closed there. The repaired armature was rebuilt into the engine at Boulogne, but it was found to be faulty as it was before it was sent to Messrs. Newton & Wright, London, for repairs. A new armature had been ordered from America, and as it had arrived, it was placed in position and gave satisfaction. The damaged armature was returned to Messrs. Newton and Wright on the 20th March for further repairs. The two X-Ray Operating Rooms were laid out as follows. One, which was well lighted, was used as a Radiographic Room, where photographs and localization of foreign bodies on plates were done. The other was made light tight and used for fluoroscopy. In it, an X-Ray couch with the tube underneath and an upright stand with counter balancing weights were placed. Both of these apparati we constructed by ourselves and found very satisfactory. A large viewing box to hold eighty plates was built on to one side of the room, and has proved a success. It has reduced the breakage of X-Ray plates, as it has reduced the handling of them. One operator can make fifty X-Ray plates in a day, working with one engine, and this number has frequently been exceeded. In order to accomplish fifty in one day, it is necessary to have:- (1) The Operator; (2) A man to develop the plates; (3) A man to look after the engines and help with patients; (4) An orderly who gets another from the Ward to help him bring the patients; (5) Two patients who act as orderlies. The distances which the patients have to be brought are great, and cause delay in getting the work done. Meal hours also are awkwardly arranged, as patients are at dinner from eleven to twelve; the orderlies from twelve to one, and the Medical Officer at lunch from one to one thirty. The work stops at four thirty p.m. again for Men's Supper, and by the time the orderlies come back (five to five-thirty) it is nearly dark in winter and early spring, and somewhat dangerous on that account to carry wounded men. After a convoy arrives, we begin at eight-thirty a.m. and go on to seven p.m., but even then as many as seventeen patients have remained not X-Rayed until next day. By using a Coolidge Tube, we have been able to dispense with one engine, and work direct from the mains, so that we have practically three X-Ray outfits at our command. The Coolidge Tube is borrowed from No. 1 Canadian General Hospital. Two new methods of localizing foreign bodies have been used. One on the assumption that all German bullets are of the same diameter, let us proceed as follows:- A small wood stand was made so that a German bullet could be placed on it and separated from a photographic plate by half inches from 0 to 6". Thirteen photographs were made of the bullet at each distance, and the result is shown on the accompanying photograph. The bullet gradually grows in size as it recedes from the plate, and its greatest diameter corresponds in each case to its distance from the plate. There is sufficient distance between each photograph to be able to say definitely at what distance the bullet lay from the plate. With this guiding photograph it was found possible to tell the depth of a German bullet to within half an inch by comparing the size of the bullet in the patient's photograph with that of the bullet at a known distance. Although this is comparatively a rough method, it has proved useful when only one photograph had been taken, as sometimes happened when we had fifty cases to do at a time after the arrival of a convoy and operation took place whether the negative was ready or not, owing to surgical necessity. The triangulation method was used for exact localization. The supply of X-Ray plates has been good all along. Very rarely has the manufacturer supplied a bad plate, but many of them have been broken in transport; we have used most of the broken plates and had satisfactory results from them. We have made twenty-nine lantern slides of some interesting cases and a few prints as required for sending away; but apart from this no unnecessary expense has been incurred by the Department. The following article has been submitted to me by Captain L. J. Rhea, Pathologist of this Unit. A SIMPLE AND EASILY MADE ACTIVE SERVICE ALCOHOL LAMP. -A SUBSTITUTE FOR THE BUNSEN BURNER. When Hospitals in the field occupy tents, huts, or permanent buildings without gas connections, some form of lamp for laboratory work has to be substituted for the ordinary Bunsen Burner. The most common types of lamps used are the Alcohol Lamp and the Automatic Bunsen Burner for Methylated Spirits. Both are useful, though both have certain disadvantages. The chief disadvantages of the Alcohol Lamp are the comparatively small amount of heat it produces and the unsteadiness of the flame. The principal objections to the Automatic Bunsen Burner for ordinary laboratory work are, that it is not easily carried about, it takes some time to get it lit, and it requires constant care lest it gets out of order. I have made an Alcohol Lamp which has certain advantages over the two common types of lamps used in Laboratories in the Field. Its chief advantages are - it can be made in the laboratory from material easily available in any hospital in the Field; the amount of heat produced is greater than the ordinary Alcohol Lamp, though not as great as the Automatic Bunsen Burner; it can be carried about as easily as an Alcohol Lamp; it takes but a moment to light; it is economical in the consumption of alcohol; the flame is steady, and the amount of heat it produces can be fairly well regulated. This lamp is made from the following:- One tin with a tight fitting cover; one rifle cartridge from which the cap has been removed, one "Pull Through", a piece of cotton tent rope, a circular piece of wood or cork, one small piece of wire, and a circular piece of tin. Any tin with a tight fitting cover can be used, though I have found a "MacKintoshes'" toffee tin the most satisfactory. This can be obtained at the British Expeditionary Force Canteen. The advantages of this tin are its size, shape, and the type of cover which is easily removed and makes the tin air-tight. It has this disadvantage - the joints are not soldered and the tin will not hold alcohol until they are. On the top of the cover place a circular piece of cork or wood one half inch in thickness. If a "MacKintoshes'" toffee tin is used this should be cut to fit into the depression in the cover. Over this place a circular piece of tin, or still better, a second "MacKintoshes'" toffee tin cover. | H.S.B. |

Cut the "Pull Through" in two parts by filing it just beyond the rim that projects into its lumen and acts as a catch for the knot on the string which passes through it. The "Pull Through" should be cut on that side of this rim that will give the longest piece of tube with a uniform calibre. Fill the longer piece with molten lead or solder. Allow this to cool, and bend one end of the tube at right angles, with the short arm of the right angle one third to one half of an inch long. Heat the tubing to remove the lead or solder. This procedure is carried out in order to ensure that there is no narrowing or distortion of the lumen, when one end of the tube is bent at a right angle. Make a wick of cotton tent rope and pass it through the tube, made from the "Pull Through". This wick should fit snugly. Into the end of the rifle cartridge, insert a snugly fitting flat end wick made from cotton tent rope, and pass it into the cartridge for a short distance. The end of the wick should not reach closer than one and a half inches from the base of the cartridge. The wicks of the "Pull Through" and the cartridge should be of sufficient length to reach to the bottom of the tin used to hold the alcohol. Insert the "Pull Through" with its wick and the cartridge with its wick through the hole made for them in the cover. Fill the tin with Methylated Spirits and put the cover with its attachments in place. Light the wick of the bent "Pull Through". By these means, I have been able to hold a water bath at 56 degrees centigrade for eight hours with a variation of less than half a degree. I have used French, English and German rifle cartridges. The French and English cartridges that I have seen have two holes in their base through which the spark from the cap ignites the explosive, and as a result, gives a lower and broader flame than the German cartridge I have used, which has only one such hole. A high and narrow flame may be obtained from either the English or French cartridge by plugging one of the holes in its base with a Gramaphone needle or the end of a pin. If a German cartridge, or an English or French cartridge with only one of the holes in their base left open is used, it may be necessary to enlarge them a little, if a comparatively large flame is desired. Unless this is done, the volatilized alcohol may escape under a pressure sufficient to blow out the flame. When it is desired to put the flame out, drop a small tin, or the cap of an alcohol lamp over both burners. Plasticine placed so that the rim of this cover rests upon it, will ensure that there is no evaporation of alcohol when the lamp is not in use. The flame produced by this lamp is almost colourless, and, as all flames from volatilized inflammable materials escaping under pressure, very hot. I have found this lamp to fulfil satisfactorily all the ordinary requirements of a Bunsen Burner. | H.S.B. |
