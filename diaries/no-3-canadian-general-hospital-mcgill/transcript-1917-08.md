## Transcription conventions
- Faithful to the original: spelling, punctuation, abbreviations and capitalisation as written. Typewritten on Army Form C. 2118, "WAR DIARY or INTELLIGENCE SUMMARY", in columns: Place | Date | Hour | Summary of Events and Information | Remarks and references to Appendices.
- `[uncertain]` marks a word or passage that could not be read with confidence — never guessed. One `[uncertain]` mark was needed this month, in the printed footer on diary-text p. 1, where the "Wt." contract reference is too faint to read; see the open questions below.
- As in March and June, the sub-heading "INTELLIGENCE SUMMARY" IS struck through, with "or" handwritten above it — the heading reads "WAR DIARY or INTELLIGENCE SUMMARY", with "(Erase heading not required.)" beneath it.
- The Remarks column throughout is the handwritten initials "H.S.B." (Colonel H. S. Birkett). The statistics table and the discipline paragraph on diary-text p. 3 also carry "H.S.B." in the Remarks column, and "H.S.B." stands in the Remarks column beside the close of Lt-Colonel Elder's surgical summary on Agenda p. 6.
- The oval Office of Officer Commanding stamp ("OFFICE OF OFFICER COMMANDING / 31 AUG. 1917 / No. 2—WD—8—17 / No. 3 Canadian General Hospital—McGill") appears on the cover and on all diary-text and Agenda pages.
- No hours are recorded in August — the Hour column is blank throughout.
- Unlike June, August DOES give monthly patient figures — but in the diary text itself, in the statistics table on diary-text p. 3: 5,109 patients received during August (2,909 surgical, 1,307 medical, 893 detained). No deaths, discharges or operations totals are given anywhere in the volume.
- The Place column is entered only three times: "Boulogne" against 1 August and 8 August (diary-text p. 1) and against 17 August (diary-text p. 2); the hospital remained at Boulogne all month. The Agenda is filed with Place "Boulogne" and Date "1" in the columns on its first page.
- 28 August has two entries: the gale on diary-text p. 2, and its continuation (the 521 patients admitted from other hospitals) on diary-text p. 3; both are transcribed under their own page.
- The Agenda runs over ten pages (August 1917, p. 6–15) and is transcribed as continuous text, not as a table. > The following is a brief, but extremely interesting summary of the surgical work as carried on by Lt. Colonel John M. Elder, CAMC, O., i/c Surgical Division of the hospital under my command, which I think is worthy of being placed on record:—
>
> "Since this hospital began work in France in 1915, between sixty-five and seventy thousand patients have been treated, and a statement with regard to the surgical treatment of patients in this hospital might be of some value to the Service.
>
> (1) CONDITION OF SURGICAL PATIENTS COMING TO US FROM THE FRONT.
>
> [Underlined.]
>
> So far as my observations go, I am bound to say that there has been a progressive, and in many cases a noted, improvement in the condition of the wounds arriving at the Base; and this improvement is undoubtedly attributable to better knowledge, and more careful technique in the Field Ambulances and Casualty Clearing Stations. While this statement is true, there is still room for improvement. The chief criticism to be made of the treatment at the Front is want of adequate drainage, and this, I take it, is due mainly to two causes, viz.,
>
> (i) The proper effort is not always made to secure dependant drainage, which is so essential when these cases have to be transferred to the Base.
>
> (ii) The principles governing the use of Carrell's tubes, salt packs, B.I.P. and other forms of drainage for wounds, appear, in some instances, not to be sufficiently understood.
>
> Patients frequently arrive here with any of these three forms of drainage, packed in on top of some septic foreign body. Moist dressings and drainage gauze are also often found in wounds which have been treated with B.I.P. But, on the whole, the treatment of wounds at the Front is infinitely better than it was in 1915, and it is a rare thing now for us to see septic knee joint filled with rubber drainage tubes, or septic abdomens with a drainage tube leading down to a faecal fistula.

> (2) CHANGES IN SURGICAL TREATMENT AT OUR OWN HOSPITAL.
>
> [Underlined.]
>
> Ninety per cent of war surgery, as illustrated in our work here, consists in combating sepsis in one form or another. To do this, two things are necessary:—
>
> (i) That the wounds shall be cleaned and drained.
>
> (ii) That healthy granulation shall, as speedily as possible, replace sloughs. Under the second heading will come, of course, all amputations, which should only be resorted to when attempts to save the limb might involve the loss of the patient.
>
> We have at our disposal two main methods of combating sepsis:—
>
> (i) The use of some antiseptic; and this war has produced more so called antiseptics than all the previous history of surgery has done. We began by irrigating each wound with Eusol and Dakin's fluid, hypertonic salines, five per cent solution of Ether and warm water, the use of B.I.P — and partial closure of the wound, and lately the use of Flavine (one to five thousand). Each wound, is more or less a law to itself, and some one of the antiseptics mentioned may be preferable to any one of the others in the treatment of certain wounds. Eusol, Flavine, and hypertonic salines will clean up a sloughing wound, especially if the bath be used, in shorter time than anything else. Menciere's fluid, or five per cent Ether, promote granulations better than Eusol or Flavine, and once these granulations have formed, if B.I.P can be applied, and the wound partially closed, the healing is more rapid than by any other method we have used, and what is of greater importance still, the patient has painless and less frequent dressings. — So much for the antiseptics which we have added to our armamentarium.
>
> (ii) We must, in very many instances, notably in the case of the large serous cavities, rely upon phagocytosis to control sepsis; and our efforts here will be directed towards securing an abundant output of healthy leucocytes in order to overcome the bacteria.
