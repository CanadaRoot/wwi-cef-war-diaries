## Page 6 — Diary text, p. 3 (October 1915, p. 6)

> PAGE THREE. — WAR DIARY or INTELLIGENCE SUMMARY — Army Form C. 2118
>
> I beg to make note of the following report from L.Colonel J.M.Elder, Officer i/c of Surgery:—
> 1. Number of patients admitted to Surgical Wards — 1650; total number of deaths in Surgical Wards — 36; total number of operations done in Operating Theatre — 450; Total number of deaths in patients operated upon — 21.
> 2. These excellent results are due to the good work done by all the members of my staff, especially to the excellent technique and good judgment of Majors Hill and Archibald, who have done most of the operating, assisted by the different Captains i/c of Wards. As an example of this I might mention the very gratifying results we have had in the treatment of septic knee joints. Of these we have had at least 25 cases, of which we have only had to resort to amputation of the limb in two cases, and seven cases, treated by aspiration and injections of formaline and glycerine, we were able to return to England within a month with good moveable joints. These results I have never seen equalled in civil surgery. I would also like to mention the ingenuity displayed by the M.O.'s i/c of Wards, notably Captains Turner, Kingston, and Reford, in adapting and inventing special splints for special kinds of fractures.
> 3. The Anaesthetic Department, under the guidance of Captain Howell, has given perfect satisfaction. Chloroform Anaesthesia, by means of the "Vernon Harcourt" Inhaler has been used in the majority of cases. The Gas-Oxygen apparatus has been most useful in desperate, or very short, cases and the portable Nitrous Oxide apparatus has enabled members of the staff to do, painlessly, many dressings in the wards, which would otherwise have proved agonising to the patients. Spinal Anaesthesia has also been very satisfactory, and I am glad to report that the ampoules of stovaine we brought with us from Montreal are proving absolutely reliable.
> 4. Our indebtedness to Captain Rhea and the Pathological Staff is very great. By the rapid methods of staining in use in the Laboratory, we can know, in a very short time, the exact nature of the infection present in any given wound, and are thus enabled to treat every case intelligently. Further, we depend absolutely on Captain Rhea's reports as to the asepticity of all the material used in the Operating Room, and he has also, by several plate cultures, determined for us the adequacy of our methods of disinfecting the room after doing a series of septic cases. Altogether the work of this Department is quite as good as we have been accustomed to get from Captain Rhea at home, and that is saying very much.
> 5. I must again express my appreciation of the valuable aid rendered to us by Captains Pirie and Wilkins in the X-Ray Department, not only by very clear skiagrams (often taken directly on paper), but also by the very accurate localisation of foreign bodies, the removal of which forms such a large part of our routine operations.
> 6. I also desire to commend the work of the Operating Room Staff, both Sisters and Orderlies. [continued on p. 4]

> [Rhea's laboratory report, continued:] complete blood counting tray. By this arrangement, blood counts and stains of blood films can be made in the wards without the inconvenience and loss of time necessitated by a trip to the laboratory. >
> Clinical Laboratory. This laboratory contains all the technical equipment necessary for the satisfactory examination of the materials sent to it, and I am informed by the clinicians that the work done in it compares favourably with that done in a well equipped laboratory in civil life. The routine work of the Clinical Laboratory is done for the most part by the Ward Masters, who are either recent graduates in Medicine or students in their final years. Such an arrangement has at least two advantages: it relieves the Medical Officers in charge of the Wards from much clinical laboratory work thus giving them more time available for the study of the cases under their care, and it gives the Ward Masters certain definite responsibilities. This work done under the direction of an experienced laboratory man would seem to be as trustworthy as if the Medical Officers had done it themselves. We are prepared to do — and are doing, routine examinations of urine, including quantitative analysis; gastric analysis including quantitative examinations; complete sputum examinations; examinations of faeces including the examination for occult blood; blood examinations including haemoglobin estimations and differential cell counts. The above examinations here, as in many civil Hospitals, have now become a definite part of the Hospital work. We are prepared to make more complicated clinical laboratory tests when the emergencies arise. Besides the assistance rendered to the clinician, which we have always kept foremost in our minds, there have been a number of interesting observations made in the clinical laboratory. Among them I will mention the following:— the frequency of albumen in the urine of the soldiers under your care. One case is worthy of especial mention on account of the very high per cent of albumen found in his urine. Several cases of infection with malarial parasites have been observed. The interest in these cases lies, not so much in the mere presence of the malarial organisms as in the fact that certain of the infected soldiers had never been outside of England before the present war. > [Rhea's laboratory report, continued:] smears and the examination of cultures. We have met with considerable difficulty in the examination of cultures for the reason that many of the infecting organisms are anaerobic, practically always in impure culture. Anaerobic work offers difficulties even in a well equipped laboratory in civil life. We realise the difficulties and possibilities of mistakes of diagnosis from direct smears especially when we are dealing with infecting organisms which are more or less unfamiliar to us. In so far as we can learn, we have not advised conservative treatment at the sacrifice of a life or even a limb, and we have been fortunate enough to see more than one case of "Gas Infection" of the tissues detected bacteriologically and operated upon in time to save both limb and life. Our present idea, which may at any time be changed, in regard to the bacteriological study of the wounds inflicted in this war, may be briefly summarized as follows — practically all wounds inflicted by shrapnel are heavily infected. On the other hand one not infrequently sees sterile wounds inflicted by bullets. It seems to us that many of the organisms found in the wounds are of comparatively low virulence and that not infrequently these organisms grow in the necrotic tissues lining the track of the wounds and do not spread into the more nearly normal tissues beyond. The presence of bubbles of gas escaping from a wound, does not of necessity mean the presence of the "Gas Bacillus", and the presence of these organisms in the discharges does not necessarily mean infection of the tissue with them, any more than the presence of tubercle bacilli in a lymph node means the infection of the tissues about this node.
>
> Typhoid Group. In so far as the opportunity allowed we have studied certain aspects of this group. The general outline of this work has already been presented to you and at a later date I will furnish you with the result of the work now in hand. From a report which you recently gave me, you will see that all the cases of infection with members of the Typhoid group, where an opportunity was given us to examine them, were diagnosed before they were sent to the Isolation Hospital.
>

> THORAX CASES. We have made a bacteriological and cytological examination of a number of these cases. Most of them are sterile. One has been of especial interest — a case infected with an anaerobic bacillus. This infecting organism was found in the smears from the fluid removed and has been cultivated in pure culture. One interesting point in this case was the very active phagocytosis of the organisms.
>
> SPECIAL EXAMINATIONS FOR THE CHIEF SURGEON. We have made certain bacteriological examinations for Lt. Colonel Elder of materials used in the Operating Room and in no instance have we found any evidence of infection of the material given us. In these examinations both aerobic and anaerobic cultures were made. We have recently exposed plates in various parts of the Operating room and have studied the organisms recovered. The number and kind of organisms per plate exposed during the working hours of the Operating Room compare favourably with an Operating Room in civil life. We have also examined the sweepings from the floor, taken after a series of operations; none of the cultures showed the "Gas Bacillus". The comparatively low count of bacteria recovered from the exposed plates may in part be due to the dressing used on the floor.
>
> MOSQUITOES. We have not made a careful survey of the mosquitoes of this district but we have examined a few that have come to us. None of these belong to the malarial bearing mosquitoes. If the opportunity presents itself we hope to make a more careful study of this subject.
>
> VACCINES. Soon after the laboratory was opened we notified the Medical Officers that we carried certain stock vaccines and were prepared to make autogenous vaccines when requested. A number of cases have already been treated with, on the whole, good results.
>
> HARVEST MITES. There recently occurred among the men of the Unit under your command, cases of an irritating skin lesion. We were able to find in several of them a very small mite, commonly known as the "Harvest Mite".
>
> EMERGENCY APPARATUS. The emergencies of active service have required certain technical apparatus that was not available. In most instances we have been able to meet the emergency by means that are already familiar to you.
>
> POST MORTEM EXAMINATIONS. We have held a Post Mortem examination on most of the cases that have died in the wards of this Unit. In so far as possible, these Post Mortems have been done at a time most convenient to the Medical Officers under whose care the case was. By this means we have tried to carry on the Post Mortem so that the greatest benefit to all concerned would be derived.

> [Memorandum, continued — the O.C.'s remarks on the ambulances and on the hospital's condition:]
>
> I have, in connection with this Unit, gifts of four Ambulances kindly subscribed for, one by Mrs. Huntley Drummond, one by Mrs. W. R. Miller — both of Montreal —, one by the citizens of Montreal through Lady Allan, and one by the citizens of Quebec through Mrs. Turner, all given to me personally. The expense of maintenance and repairs being entirely borne by a fund contributed to by these people. These Ambulances have been of untold service to this Unit and have also been available at the call of the R.M.O. of this area for all convoys and evacuations occurring in connection with the various Hospitals in this area. The work which these Ambulances have carried out will be gathered from the following statement:— During the month of August 61 runs were made and a total of 269 patients carried, these being divided up between our own Hospital, No. 20, 22, and 25 General Hospitals of this District. In the month of September these Ambulances made 142 runs and conveyed 844 patients. During October they made 146 runs and carried 932 patients. In addition to the aforementioned Hospitals No. 18 General also participated in these privileges. Thus for three months these Ambulances made 350 runs and have carried 2035 patients, and all this work has been done without any expense to the Government whatsoever. The Ambulances are in the care of expert chauffeurs, whose work in civil life is the same, and it has all been done in a most satisfactory manner and without a single accident.
>
> | Place | Date | Hour | Summary of Events and Information | Remarks |
> | Camiers. | Oct. 31. | | Since the memorandum of the 25th inst. the conditions enumerated under that date have been accentuated by the storm of to-day. It has also been further impressed upon my mind that with such conditions as these the patients are not receiving all that is due to them. In the matter of transferring patients, who require operative interference, from the ward to the Operating theatre and back again, this has to be carried out under most unfavourable conditions. The exposure during transit and the subsequent anaesthesia, which they have to undergo, adds, under these circumstances, very considerably to the degree of shock, and I am firmly convinced that the conditions as they at present are, with no solution of the difficulties at present in sight, that under the circumstances this situation is totally unsuitable for the purpose for which it was originally intended. | H.S.B. |
