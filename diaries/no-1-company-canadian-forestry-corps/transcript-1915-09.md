## Volume 1 — Narrative history (pages 4–19)

*Retrospective history of the unit, compiled in compliance with a letter from the Historical Section, Canadian Records Office, and signed by Capt. H. Hellmuth on 20 October 1917 (see page 19). Transcribed in full below, in the diary's own order.*

### Page 4 — (1) Introduction; mobilisation, April 1915

> WAR — DIARY
>
> No 1 CANADIAN FOREST COY.
>
> (Compiled in compliance with letter Historical Section, Canadian Records Office dated 1st March 1917)
>
> In April 1915 there were three squadrons mobilized at St. Johns, Quebec for reinforcements to the original Remount Squadron, under Lieut. Col. Wm. Hendrie, then in England. These Squadrons were known as A, B and C and were in training at the barracks under the command of Col. Pischie [uncertain]. A and C Squadrons were composed of men from all parts of Canada but chiefly from Eastern Canada — Ontario, Quebec, Nova Scotia, New Brunswick, Prince Edward Island etc. B Squadron was at first entirely composed of Western men picked on account of their horsemanship — chiefly from the Plains — Manitoba, Saskatchewan, Alberta and British Columbia. Some of the men comprising

### Page 5 — (2) B Squadron sails, 10 May 1915

> these squadrons had had previous military experience in volunteer corps throughout the Dominion, but the majority were raw recruits of an exceptionally intelligent class, having been on their own resources the greater part of their lives and skilled in some trade or profession besides being excellent horsemen.
>
> In the beginning of May 1915 it was made known that B Squadron under Major Arnold was to be brought up to strength by drafting men in from A and C, preparatory to proceeding Overseas. By this time the men had experienced three weeks to one month's training and discipline, but no horses had been issued up to this time.
>
> On May 10th 1915, B Squadron sailed from Montreal on the S. S. Cameronia belonging to the Anchor Line. Major Arnold had with him as Officers Lt. Col. Lynch-Stanton, Lt. Tripp and Lt. H. Hellmuth and 181 N.C.O's and men. The trip across was uneventful — very even weather prevailing.

### Page 6 — (3) Plymouth and Romsey, 20 May–5 June 1915

> We arrived at Plymouth on May 20th and disembarked the same day leaving by train immediately for Romsey (Hants). At Romsey where there was a large Imperial Remount Depot, the Squadron was billetted for two days, going into Camp under canvas on the 23rd May. From May 23rd until June 5th the Squadron — Officers and Men — underwent a course of training in Imperial methods, which proved of extreme interest at the time, and usefulness at a later date. On our arrival at Romsey we were informed that the Squadron commanded by Col. Hendrie had already proceeded to France. Our men were very popular with the Imperials and the Officers from the Commandant to the most junior Sub. could not do enough for us making life in and out of the Mess very pleasant, and I think I am safe in stating that all the Officers of B Squadron (which after arriving in France was called

### Page 7 — (4) Romsey; breaking horses

> the 101 Squadron) greatly appreciated the courtesy and kindness extended to us as Canadians.
>
> It was at Romsey that our men had their first chance of tackling really bad horses and I think our methods were a source of great surprise. Sgt. Smith I am sure will be remembered by British Officers, who were at Romsey during our stay, for years to come. Deplanche, Flint, Horton, and others too numerous to mention gave a fine account of themselves and incidentally as good an exhibition of horsemanship as one will see in any "round up" in Canada, or the United States. On these occasions it was remarkable to notice how many Squadron Commanders and Junior Officers had business over towards the fields where the bad horses were being broken, and I know the Commandant Col. Jessop was immensely impressed with the lack of brutality that sometimes accompanies this work.
>
> On June 5th 1915 we embarked at

### Page 8 — (5) Southampton to Dieppe, 5–6 June 1915; 101 Squadron; Director of Works letter, 25 September 1915

> Southampton for France, arriving at Dieppe on the morning of June 6th. We immediately proceeded to report our arrival to Col. Hendrie then in command of the Canadian Remounts at Harlot. Here we became the 101 Squadron, the original Squadron being known as the 100, and at a later date were joined by reinforcements from St. Johns making up 102 Squadron (Three Squadrons in all). Information from this date until September 1915, regarding our movements, work, and various duties, mode of living etc., will be fully chronicled, no doubt by the then Commanding Officer.
>
> On Sep 25th 1915 the Director of Works wrote the Director of Remounts as follows:— "It is desired to organise No 1 Forest Party as soon as possible for work in Rouvray Forest near Rouen, and it is desired to obtain the assistance of 1 Officer and 35 other ranks (lumbermen) from the Canadian Remount Depot at Harlot. Can you arrange

>

### Page 10 — (7) Choosing the men for the Forest Party

> previously for this work from the Remounts and when I came to choose my N.C.O's and men I had between 100 and 150 to choose from. I was therefore able to secure the services of men representing about every trade desired. For lumbering operations on at all an extensive scale 35 men alone could hardly show results. I therefore decided that labour (i.e. ordinary labour of some kind) would probably be furnished and with the meagre information at hand I took a chance and picked my men rather for their intelligence, good character, and knowledge of such things, as booking, Blacksmith, Millwright, Sawyers, Saw Sharpeners, Mechanics & Engineer (Stationary and Locomotive), Chauffeur, Gas Engineer, Teamsters, and two or three who had actually felled timber — but with the exception of possibly one I don't think there was a real Canadian lumberjack in the Party. Nevertheless they were a fine, well set up, clean cut lot, representing Canada from the Maritime Provinces to British Columbia. No discrimination was made with regard to locality. As it turned

### Page 11 — (8) Dieppe to Rouen, 29 September 1915; A.S.C. Labour Company; Rouvray Forest

> out I was fortunate in my choice in the method of securing men. It is true we were the first Canadians to cut trees under the then S.W.O. Forests. Within a week I was joined by an A.S.C. Labour Company, consisting of 1 Officer and 106 other ranks. These men were unskilled in forest exploitation and did the loading of railway wagons and Horse Transport, (which consisted of an A.S.C. Horse Transport Co) both in the forest and at the Station, while the Canadians did the felling and converting.
>
> We left Dieppe on Sep 29th and arrived at Rouen the same evening. I was instructed to proceed for the night to the Glamorgan Camp, consisting of R.E's. Here we were kindly received by Capt. Hybart [uncertain] R.E. D.S.O. who along with his Junior Officers did everything possible to make us feel at home.
>
> Two days later Major Osborne R.E. D.S.O. the newly appointed S.W.O. Forest, under the Directorate of Works, and myself made an examination of the Forest of Rouvray and the proposed accommodation for the men, who were

### Page 12 — (9) Oissel; felling begins 3 October 1915; output by 1 November 1915

> billetted in the barracks of Oissel about twelve kilometres from Rouen, on the fringe of the Forest. The French troops had been billetted there previous to our occupation, and it made a very fine billet. I may mention here that for the whole year the Canadians were stationed at Oissel the greatest feeling of friendship existed between all ranks, and the French civilian population, not one single complaint being recorded. We worked in this Forest as nearly like civilians as possible. There was practically no necessity for Orderly Room, the men working 10 hours each day except Sundays, on which day passes from 10 a.m. to 10 p.m. were issued enabling the men to visit Rouen and any of the villages in its vicinity.
>
> [X in margin]
>
> The actual felling commenced on the 3rd October 1915 in the Forest of Rouvray. By 1st Nov. besides the A.S.C. Labour Coy, we had some 250 civilians working in the Forest, 14 Horse Transport Wagons, and were despatching eight to ten trucks of Defence Timber from Oissel Station each day; that is approximately from 80 to 100 tons per day.

### Page 13 — (10) April 1916: expansion at Petit Couronne and Oissel

> At the beginning of April we extended and built a siding and commenced shipments from Petit Couronne on the other side of the Forest as well. By this time I was working between 700 to 800 civilians and had a Company of R.E. Labour attached consisting of some 250 men. The Motor Transport were brought up and comprised of 8 Loydenes [uncertain] and Claytons, 2 Steam Tractors with trailers and 10 Petrol Lorries. I had installed 2 Saw Mills, one [inserted above the line] at Petit Couronne and another on the Belgian Siding at Oissel composed of Scotch Benches and capable of turning out 20,000 to 25,000 F.B.M. per day. Our shipments at this time averaging 14 cars at Petit Couronne and 30 to 36 at Oissel; in other words the despatches would be 450 to 500 tons per day.
>
> The French regulations are very severe in the French State Forests; the destruction of new growth is reduced to a minimum, also in some cases certain trees are left for regeneration purposes, and in felling great care must be exercised not to injure these trees. The French Officials at first were very apprehensive knowing our Canadian methods

### Page 14 — (11) No. 2 and No. 3 Forest Parties; company authorised, April 1916

> of exploitation but after they had watched us very closely for a week or two were satisfied and on more than one occasion we were complimented by the same Officials.
>
> Shortly after No 1 Forest Party left the Canadian Remount Depot at Harlot, No 2 Party consisting of 22 men and a Corporal were asked to commence operations in the Forest of Senarpont, and shortly afterwards No 3 Forest Party was asked for; this party consisting of one Corporal and some 20 other ranks went to the Forest of Brettone. These three parties comprising some 80 men were on loan to the Imperial forces under the Director of Works.
>
> In April 1916 the Canadian Remounts at Harlot were disbanded leaving the three parties on detachment in forests, without any establishment and in order to overcome this difficulty the Director of Works, being anxious to retain the services of the Canadians on forestry work asked that the detachments might be formed into one Company, having an Establishment of its own. This was granted — authority War Office letter 121-6644 (D.F. W dated 28.9.16) and the establishment of the Company was based on the letter as follows:—

> Head Quarters.

> Having completed the work allotted by the French in Rouvray Forest on Sep. 28th 1916, the Company moved to the Forest of St. Evroult leaving two parties on detachment consisting of 10 men to superintend the finishing of the mill work, the loading and the final dismantling of the Saw Mills.
>
> The Forest of St. Evroult consisted of some twenty two thousand cubic metres of Saw and Defence Timber necessitating the building of five kilometres of Metre Gauge Railway Line, the operation of three Mills, which were on hire from a French Contractor, and the various lumbering operations connected with the exploitation. For this work it was hoped besides the 1st Canadian Forest Coy and Horse Transport Detachment of 18 horses for logging

### Page 17 — (14) St. Evroult, to March–May 1917; move to Crecy, 14 September 1917

> purposes, that civilian labour up to 150 men would be employed, and at first it was possible to maintain the civilians up to this strength. Towards the end of March 1917, the French Authorities made it very difficult to employ this class of labour, and on the 5th May a Detachment of Scottish Rifles consisting of 2 Officers and 200 men were attached for loading and Mill work. The operation of the mills and railroad was carried out by the Canadian personnel, two locomotives, and some twenty-nine cars being constantly in operation. The loading at the main line was done at St. Evroult Station, where 15 to 20 trucks were loaded each day. In one month besides despatching scantling and planking we were able to supply some 30,000 railway sleepers sawn in the mills; also some one thousand tons of defence timber, pit-props, and poles. Both this Forest and that of Rouvray entirely consisted of Pine, resembling very much the Jack Pine of Canada.
>
> On the 14 September 1917 the Company moved to the Forest of Crecy having completed exploiting the timber allotted in the Forest of St. Evroult.

### Page 18 — (15) Lt. Haight attached, 9 July 1917; drafts to Infantry and R.F.C.; Crecy Forest

> On the 9th July 1917 Lt. M. C. Haight from the Canadian Base, Etaples, and belonging to the 4th Canadian Battalion was attached for duty to No 1 Canadian Forest Company.
>
> Since the Unit received its establishment, drafts have been sent to the Infantry up to the number of fifteen and five to the Royal Flying Corps.
>
> The No 1 Canadian Forest Company is working entirely under the Imperial Branch of Forestry in France, coming directly under the D.D. of T. L of C Group, and has no connection with the Canadian Forestry Corps which was formed some six to eight months after the No 1 Canadian Forest Company had been operating in France, and it was chiefly due to the efforts of the N.C.O's and men of the 1st Canadian Forest Coy, that the Director of Works suggested to the War Office, securing the services of lumber men from Canada.
>
> The Company is working at present in Crecy Forest which consists chiefly of beech

### Page 19 — (16) Crecy Forest work; officers; signature, 20 October 1917

> and hornbeam. Besides exploiting, felling, and transporting defence timber, there is a mill turning out road slabs and planking. Charcoal burning is being carried on for supplies to the trenches during the winter months. There will be some ten kilometres of railroad to be constructed, linking up with the present Metre Gauge Line (belonging to the State) running through the Forest, and the work will very likely last from eight to twelve months.
>
> The Officer Commanding is Capt. H. Hellmuth, comes from Toronto, Canada. The Officer attached is Lt. M. C. Haight from Niagara Falls, Ontario. Prior to the War Capt. Hellmuth had no service; Lt. Haight had 10 years service in the Canadian Militia.
>
> For Pay and Records this Unit comes under the Canadian Government.
>
> The chief usefulness of the Company in the last two years has been the number of men on detachment in the various forests in the capacity of instructors etc., and there is one corporal (Corpl Le Duc) who since the beginning of operations has never been with his Unit, being in charge of contractors [at] Crecy Forest, work entirely on his own.
>
> [Stamp, lower left: CRECY FOREST, L. OF C. GROUP. No.......... Date 20-10-17]
>
> [Signed] H. Hellmuth, Capt. O.C. No 1 Can. Forest Coy.
